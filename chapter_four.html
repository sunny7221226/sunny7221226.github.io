Chapter Four

Harsh memory - continue

Susan’s sister stayed only a week. Her parents remained longer, nearly three months, living in the old house alongside the tenants. For them, everything in Canada still felt temporary and unfamiliar—the weather, the silence between houses, the endless distances driven without conversation. They never complained. If something made them uncomfortable, they adjusted quietly around it, the way they always had. Susan visited often with the children. Sometimes after work, sometimes on weekends, sometimes only for an hour so the girls could eat dinner with their grandparents before bedtime. The visits mattered to her more than she expected. Watching her parents sit at the same table as her daughters created a brief feeling of continuity, as if parts of her life that had been separated by oceans were finally touching again. Ryane noticed. “You’re taking them there a lot,” he said one evening. His tone was casual on the surface, but underneath it was the familiar accounting she had learned to recognize—time measured, movements tracked, balance evaluated. So Susan adjusted. She tried to divide the children’s time more carefully, to avoid another argument before it fully formed. One evening at her parents’ place, one evening at home. Weekends shortened. Visits explained in advance. The calculations exhausted her, but avoiding conflict still felt easier than confronting it directly. The reunion passed faster than she imagined it would. After three months, her parents decided to return home. They said they had stayed long enough. There were things waiting for them back there—routine, familiarity, obligations that had not disappeared simply because they had crossed an ocean temporarily. The day she drove them to the airport, the goodbye unfolded the way goodbyes always did in her family. No long embrace. No dramatic words. Her mother adjusted her bag strap. Her father reminded her to drive carefully. Her sister stood beside them quietly. Affection existed, but rarely in visible forms. Emotion was managed through practicality, compressed into gestures so small they could be mistaken for nothing. Susan watched them walk toward security until they disappeared into the moving crowd. Then she turned and walked back alone. The sadness did not arrive immediately. It built slowly during the drive home, settling into her chest with increasing weight until she realized she could no longer see clearly through the windshield. Tears came suddenly and continuously after that, silent but impossible to stop. She cried almost the entire drive back. Not loudly. Not violently. Just steadily, the way water keeps flowing once something holding it back has finally given way. Before pulling into the driveway, she sat in the car for a moment and wiped her face carefully with tissues from the console mirror. She checked herself once more before stepping out. Inside the house, Ryane looked at her briefly and understood immediately. “What happened?” he asked. Then, before she answered, a small sarcastic smile appeared. “They leaving forever or something?” The comment was light enough to deny if challenged. Susan said nothing. She walked past him into the kitchen, placing her keys down carefully so her hands wouldn’t shake visibly. Later that night, unable to sleep, she messaged a friend back home—someone she had known long before Canada, before marriage, before all the careful self-monitoring that now shaped every conversation. Susan told her pieces of what had been happening. Not everything, but enough. The reply came after a while. Simple. Direct. “You need to think about it,” her friend wrote. “Who knows your house that well that they could access your room so easily?” Susan stared at the message for a long time. The question itself was obvious. That was what made it difficult to escape. Because once asked plainly, the possibilities narrowed on their own.

After her parents left, Susan became more meticulous. She began noticing details she had previously ignored. A door left open by a few inches. A chair moved slightly from where she remembered leaving it. Objects placed differently on a counter. The small changes were not significant by themselves. Any one of them could be explained. She knew that. So she stopped relying on individual incidents. Instead, she began looking for patterns. One afternoon, while cleaning the bedroom, she noticed something beside the floor vent. A small cut in the carpet. It was so slight that she might easily have missed it. The edges were raised just a little, as though the carpet had been cut and then pressed back into place. The cut was close to the vent—not directly over it, but near enough that the relationship seemed deliberate. Susan crouched beside it. She touched the raised edge with one finger. There was no obvious explanation. No loose wire. No visible damage beneath the carpet. Nothing that told her what the cut was for. She stood up, then crouched again. The more she looked at it, the less accidental it seemed. That night, she began experimenting. She placed an open book on the floor beside the vent, positioning one edge close to the cut in the carpet. It was not a trap exactly. She had no clear idea what she was trying to detect. She only wanted to know whether something moved there when she was asleep. She turned off the lights. The house became quiet. For a long time, nothing happened. Then, sometime during the night, she heard a sound. A faint scrape. She opened her eyes. The book had shifted. Not far. Only a little. She stared at it in the darkness. Perhaps it had moved because of the airflow. Perhaps the floor was uneven. Perhaps she had placed it badly. She reset it the next night. The same thing happened again. After that, Susan tried different methods. She placed objects in specific positions. She marked their locations with tiny pieces of tape. She arranged things so that any movement would be visible in the morning. She tried to stay awake, telling herself she would only need to remain conscious for a few hours. But sleep always came. It came despite her efforts. She drank coffee. Took naps earlier in the day. Kept the lights on longer. Sat upright against the headboard until her neck hurt. Eventually, her eyes closed. And when she woke, the night was gone. She tried using the camera. At first, she believed the camera might finally answer the question without requiring her to remain awake. She positioned it carefully, making sure the vent and the surrounding floor were within view. The next morning, she checked the recording. Nothing. No movement. No person. No explanation. She tried again. The result was the same. Then one day the camera stopped working. She checked the battery. The charger. The memory card. Nothing helped. It was simply broken. By then, Ryane had moved out. The household changed again. Jay moved in, and later his wife came to Canada. People entered and left the house, routines shifted, rooms changed occupants. Yet the uneasiness continued. The incidents did not stop simply because the people changed. That was what troubled Susan most. If the problem had been one person, she could have understood it. If it had been a single explanation, she could have followed it to its conclusion. Instead, the house seemed to hold the uncertainty itself. The vent remained. The small cut in the carpet remained. And sometimes, late at night, when the furnace started and warm air moved upward through the floor, Susan would lie awake listening. She no longer knew whether she was waiting for proof or for sleep. Perhaps, she thought, those two things had become the same.

The idea came to her one Saturday morning. If she could not trust the room, perhaps she could change the room. The carpet had become inseparable from her fear. Every time she looked at the floor vent, every time she noticed the small cut beside it, her mind returned to the same unanswered questions. She had examined it so many times that she no longer saw a bedroom. She saw only possibilities. She decided to remove the carpet entirely. Until the work was finished, she and her younger daughter moved into the guest room. Years earlier, it had been Ryane's bedroom. After he moved out, the room had remained empty for months. Susan cleaned it thoroughly before bringing in a mattress for herself and her daughter. She had already replaced the bedroom lock and installed another bolt on the inside of the door. When she turned the bolt that first evening, the sound gave her a brief sense of relief. For the first time in weeks, she thought she might finally sleep. Sometime after midnight, she woke. Not completely. Just enough to become aware of the bed beneath her. One corner of the mattress seemed to rise gently, no more than a few centimeters, as though someone had lifted it carefully from below. Then it settled back into place. The movement lasted less than a second. Susan's eyes opened instantly. She did not move. The room was silent. Her daughter slept beside her without stirring. She remained awake until morning. The locks were still engaged. Nothing inside the room appeared disturbed. Yet the feeling that swept through her was immediate and overwhelming—not panic, but the unmistakable certainty that she was not safe. The flooring project took longer than she wanted. She worked full time, and every evening seemed to disappear into errands, meals, homework with the girls, and the slow process of preparing the room. Progress came one task at a time. When she finally pulled the carpet away from the floor, she paused. Part of her had expected to find something. A hidden opening. Altered boards. Anything that might explain why the carpet had been cut beside the vent. Instead, the wooden subfloor looked ordinary. Dust collected in the seams between the boards. Old nail holes marked previous repairs. Nothing appeared unusual. She knelt there for a long time, disappointed by how ordinary everything looked. Later, when Ryane came by to help with the renovation, he glanced across the exposed floor. "Be careful," he said. "There are probably more nails underneath." It was an ordinary remark. Helpful, even. Yet Susan found herself wondering how he knew what lay beneath the flooring. The thought embarrassed her almost immediately. Anyone might have said the same thing about an old house. Still, it stayed with her. He helped install the new flooring. Board by board, the room changed. The old carpet disappeared into garbage bags. Fresh planks covered the subfloor. The bedroom looked brighter, cleaner, almost new. Friends who saw it complimented the renovation. Susan smiled and thanked them. She wanted to believe she had reclaimed the room. She wanted the new floor to mark an ending. But underneath that hope, another thought remained. The materials were new. The room looked different. Yet the house itself had not changed. And neither had the feeling that something she did not understand continued to exist beyond what she could see. It was only later that she would begin to wonder whether replacing the floor had changed anything at all. The answer, when it came, was not the one she had hoped for.
